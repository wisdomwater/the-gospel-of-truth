## Preface

Some ancient Christian writings feel familiar almost immediately. Their language may be old, but their world is recognizable. The *Gospel of Truth* is not one of those writings. It speaks of the Pleroma and the Aeons, of Error and deficiency, of forgetfulness becoming a fog, of a living book written in the heart of the Father. At times it feels less like reading a theological argument and more like stepping into someone else's dream.

The *Gospel of Truth* was discovered among the texts of the Nag Hammadi library in Egypt in 1945, although the work itself is much older, probably dating to the second century. It is commonly associated with Valentinian Christianity, one of the diverse movements that flourished during the early centuries of the Christian tradition. Despite its name, it is not a gospel in the familiar sense. There are no stories of Jesus walking through Galilee, no collection of parables or miracles, and no narrative of his death and resurrection. It is closer to a sermon or extended meditation on what it means to know God.

Its world is not exactly our world, and I have not tried to pretend otherwise. This book is not a translation of the *Gospel of Truth*, nor is it an attempt to make Valentinian theology agree neatly with modern Christianity. It is a contemplative reading of an ancient text, listening for the spiritual movement beneath its unfamiliar language and asking what these words might sound like if their author were speaking devotionally to someone living today.

Once I began listening in that way, I found something surprisingly hopeful.

The great human problem in the *Gospel of Truth* is not described primarily as lawbreaking deserving punishment. It is described as ignorance. We have forgotten the Father, and from that forgetfulness come fear, confusion, division, and error. We become like people asleep inside a nightmare, responding to shadows as though they were the deepest reality. Salvation, then, becomes an awakening.

Again and again, the images are images of restoration. The lost sheep is sought. The sleeping are awakened. The sick are visited by a physician. What is scattered is gathered into unity, what is deficient is filled, and those who have forgotten where they came from begin to remember their home. Repentance itself becomes a turning back toward the One who has never stopped drawing near.

At the center of all of this is the Father.

For some of us, that word carries complicated associations. Christian teaching can sometimes leave us with an image of God the Father as the severe member of the Trinity: the keeper of the law, the source of judgment, the One whose anger must somehow be satisfied. Jesus then becomes the compassionate one who stands between humanity and the Father's wrath, offering himself so that an otherwise angry God can finally receive us.

But what if Jesus is not saving us from the Father?

What if Jesus is revealing the Father?

One of the most beautiful images in the *Gospel of Truth* says simply that the Father opens his heart. The Son comes forth from that openness, carrying into the world what has always existed within God. The compassion we encounter in Christ does not persuade the Father to become compassionate. It reveals that compassion was there from the beginning.

This changes something.

When Jesus welcomes the rejected, we are seeing the heart of the Father. When he touches the wounded, searches for the lost, forgives the sinner, and speaks peace to the frightened, he is not acting against God's disposition toward humanity. He is making that disposition visible. The words of mercy spoken by Jesus have their source in the One from whom Jesus came.

If you can trust Jesus, you can trust the Father.

That conviction gradually became one of the threads running through these thirty readings. Whenever the ancient language became difficult or strange, I found myself returning to the image of the Father as the Source from whom we have come and the Love toward which we are being drawn. The Father is not the enemy standing at the end of the journey. The Father is home.

This does not mean every mystery disappears. The *Gospel of Truth* itself insists that God remains beyond our ability to contain in thought. There is humility in that recognition. Every image we form of God is incomplete, including the images we inherit from our churches, our families, our fears, and these pages.

Perhaps the spiritual life is partly the work of allowing inadequate images of God to fall away. The tyrant may need to disappear. The distant judge may need to soften. The God we imagined watching from far away may need to give way to the Father who has been nearer than our own breath.

These readings are an invitation into that possibility. They are meant to be taken slowly, perhaps one each day, without any need to master the ancient cosmology behind them. Read them as invitations to remember, to awaken, and to notice where fear has created distance between you and the Love in which you already live.

The *Gospel of Truth* begins with joy. It moves through forgetfulness and fear, through awakening and healing, through wandering and return, until finally it comes to rest in the goodness of the Father. That seems like a worthy journey for us as well.

Perhaps, somewhere along the way, we may discover that the God we have spent so much of our lives searching for has been searching for us.

And perhaps we may finally allow ourselves to be found.
